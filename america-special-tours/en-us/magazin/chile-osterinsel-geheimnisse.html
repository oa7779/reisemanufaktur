The secrets of Easter Island

Small island, big questions: Easter Island is surrounded by myths and mysteries. In complete seclusion the rise and decline of a high culture took place, whose testimonies amaze laymen as well as archaeologists

How was Easter Island inhabited?

Although Easter Island is officially part of Chile, it lies amidst the expanses of the Pacific Ocean – and the mother soul alone. The nearest inhabited piece of land is over 200 kilometers away. The first question that scientists are concerned with is therefore: How could people ever reach the isolated island? At least one roughly agrees when the settlement must have taken place – about 1500 years ago.

Most researchers assume that the natives of Rapa Nui, as they call the island itself, reached by boat from Polynesia. A legend of the islanders tells of King Hotu Matua, who climbs canoes after a fight with his attachment and sails out to sea – until he finally arrives on Easter Island after a long journey. However, there are also theories according to which the first islanders came from today's Peru. As evidence of this thesis, the ceremony site Ahu Vinapu is used, whose precise construction is reminiscent of early buildings from the region.

What about the stone statues?

Most people, however, connect with Easter Island primarily the monumental stone figures that line the island to hundreds. The proud faces of the so-called Moai rise up to ten meters high – and give rise to many questions. What was the purpose of these statues, how did a people manage to make, transport and set up this tons - of - weight colossus hundreds of years ago, even putting on hats made of volcanic rock?

The Moai are clear traces of a culture that was so developed that they could afford to put a lot of resources and labor into the megalomane project to produce hundreds of huge sculptures. Most likely, the statues were part of places of worship that served ancestor worship.

What broke the high culture of Easter Island?

The Moai are also traces of a culture that broke – perhaps by their megalomania. Most of the ceremonial sites where the Moai are located were destroyed when the first Europeans arrived, and hardly any of the Moai were still standing. Although some earthquakes may have been killed, some stone statues seem to have been deliberately thrown over. In the crater of the extinct volcano Rano Raraku, where the quarry where the figures were made was located, several unfinished moais were found, among them one that would have been completed about 20 meters high.

Why the islanders moved away from their custom and even destroyed their own cultural treasures is in the dark. Some researchers assume that a change of power has brought greater importance to birdman worship and thus dropped the old belief: When the swallows laid their eggs on the small, offshore islands, the chiefs of the tribes met here for a contest about who could bring the first egg to the mainland. He then enjoyed special attention as a "bird man".

Numerous rock carvings on the Easter Island show representations of mythical bird beings. However, there are also finds that argue that the birdman and moai cult were practiced parallelly for at least a while.

What changed the landscape of the island?

Much suggests that the changes in the culture of Easter Island are due to drastic changes in nature. Those who travel to Easter Island today encounter a landscape of rugged beauty: the rugged coasts fall into the sea as steep cliffs in many places, three extinct volcanoes characterise the nature of the island and have produced various lava tubes in the ground.

The volcanic island has always been characterized by its species poverty: the distance from the mainland is too large for seeds to be brought here. However, it has not always been as sparsely covered as it is today. It is hard to imagine, but where today rocky grasslands cover the island, once dense palm forests grew, which must not only have been an important source of wood production, but also protected the island from soil erosion.

Exploitation or similar massive interventions could have put the ecosystem out of balance – and thus destroyed the basis for the extraction of food and resources. Behind this disturbance of natural balance could be a reason for the decline of the culture of the Easter Islands.

The history of Easter Island unites the great themes of humanity on a small island: culture and nature, ascent and fall, size and megalomania. For historically interested visitors, it is undoubtedly one of the most exciting destinations in the world.

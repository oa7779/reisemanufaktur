Day 1 Welcome to Christchurch

Kia Ora! You will reach Christchurch today. This starts your rental car tour. Upon arrival at the airport, pick up your rental car and drive to your accommodation. Your room stands out 14 The clock is available. Use the rest of the day to relax or explore the city if you are still enterprising despite jet lag. For a hundred years, Christchurch was characterised by beautiful gardens and parks as well as impressive architecture – much of it reminds of England. Unfortunately, an earthquake fell. 2011 Sacrificing many of the city’s treasures. Work is still being done on reconstruction. The largest city on the South Island is a vibrant, cosmopolitan place with exciting festivals and theaters, diverse shopping and other attractions. For example, you can take the modern Gondola (cable car) up to Mount Cavendish (optional). In four-person cabins you reach an altitude of approx. 500m and from there can be a breathtaking 360-Degrees to enjoy panoramic views over the Southern Alps, the Port Hills and Lyttelton, the port of Christchurch. But you can also visit the Antarctic Center or take a walk in the beautiful botanical garden. Overnight in Christchurch.

Day 2 Christchurch - Akaroa

Over the mountains of the crater rim of the peninsula “Banks Peninsula” leads the journey to Akaroa. Despite British sailors proclaiming British sovereignty for the South Island in Akaroa in 1840, it was the French settlers who established a kind of colony here. Even today you can still discover the French influence in the area, which you find mainly in geographical names. There is a special feature in the bay of Akaroa: Hector dolphins live there, a rare species that only occurs here. A coastal nature reserve has been set up for these sea creatures so that the dolphins are not injured by fishing nets. In the afternoon, take a dolphin tour. Overnight in Akaroa.

Day 3 Akaroa - Oamaru

You will pass through the Banks Peninsula, wine-growing areas and the fertile Canterbury Plain, characterized by agriculture and livestock farming. You will cross the Rakaia River, known for huge salmon, and reach Oamaru further south. At the port, a colony of native Little Blue Penguins has settled. Every evening before dawn, the animals come back to land. From a grandstand, you can watch hundreds (seasonally) of penguins waddle from the sea to their nesting sites on land from a few meters away - a very amusing spectacle! Overnight in Oamaru.

4.-5. Tag Rotorua - Oamaru - Dunedin

They continue on the Pacific coast to the south. The small settlement of Moeraki invites you to a break away from the main route. Here on the beach are the mysterious stone balls of the 'Moeraki Boulders', which fascinate visitors again and again. Along the coast, you will reach the capital of Otago Province, Dunedin, founded by Scots. An important city since its founding days, an economic upswing was announced here in 1882 - the first refrigerated ship loaded with meat, took off. In 1863 there were already gas-lit streets and in 1879, following the model of the Californian cable cars, the Cable Tramway for transport to the higher parts of the city was built. A landmark of this city, the Railway Station, should not be forgotten. The floor of this Renaissance-style railway station is decorated with 725 760 mosaic stones, which form all pretty motifs. Dunedin is the "Scottish" capital of New Zealand and has the steepest road in the world. Overnight in Dunedin.

6.-7. Day from Dunedin to Te Anau in Fiordland

Lake Te Anau is a lake in the southwest of New Zealand’s South Island and a stop on the Southern Scenic Route. Its original Māori name was Te Ana-au, which means cave of swirling water. The lake occupies an area of 344 km2, making it the second largest lake in the country after Lake Taupo and the largest lake on the South Island before Lake Wakatipu. The main part of the lake runs about 65 km in north-south direction. On the west side, there are also three large fjords, which - according to their geographical location - are called North Fiord, Middle Fiord and South Fiord. At the entrance to the Middle Fiord are some small islands. This fjord branches all to the west into a north-western and a south-west arm. Since the lake lies at an altitude of 210 m above NN, but it is sometimes up to 270 m deep, parts of the water are below sea level. Overnight in Te Anau.

Day 8 - Te Anau - Queenstown

Due to its beautiful mountain location on the shores of Lake Wakatipu, the small resort of Queenstown has developed into the popular tourist center of the South Island. The long sea stretches between steeply rising mountain ranges. You can optionally take a ride on the cable car to Bob’s Peak to enjoy a magnificent view of New Zealand’s adventure capital and the surrounding mountains. Overnight in Queenstown. Our recommendation (optional) - Go in style on the more than 100-year-old steamship TSS Earnslaw, among whose passengers were already Queen Elizabeth II and Prince Philip, over Lake Wakatipu. This historic steamboat has been serving the people of Queenstown on Lake Wakatipu for over 100 years. The parts of the ship, which was commissioned by New Zealand Railways in the early 20th century, were built in Dunedin and subsequently transported by train to the lake where the ship was assembled. Today, the ship serves 11 months of the year for passenger transport between Queenstown and Walter Peak High Country Farm as well as scenic tours on Lake Wakatipu. Overnight in Queenstown.

Day 9 Into the Glacier Region of the Southern Alps

Along the west coast you drive through evergreen rainforest to the glacier region of the southern Alps. As a natural weather barrier, the very near high mountain ridge is responsible for one of the most extreme precipitation regimes in the country. Above the snow line, large masses of ice collect, forming fast-flowing valley glaciers. The approximately 12 km long Fox and Franz Josef glaciers - unique in temperate latitudes - slide down to an altitude of 300 m above sea level. Overnight at Fox Glacier.

Day 10 Greymouth on the West Coast

Greymouth (Te Reo Maori: Mawhera) is the largest city in the West Coast region on the South Island of New Zealand and the administrative headquarters of the Grey District. The city has 9528 inhabitants (2001). This means that about 20% of the West Coast residents live in Greymouth. Before European settlers came to Greymouth, the Maori had lived in the area for some time. They called it Mawhera, today another name for the Grey River and at the same time the Maori name of the city of Greymouth. The first European to visit the area was Thomas Brunner in 1846. He discovered coal in the Greytal. His name was the godfather for the city of Brunner and Lake Brunner. Brunner named the river Grey after prominent New Zealand politician Sir George Edward Grey. Overnight in Greymouth.

11th day Wildlife up close in Kaikoura

Kaikoura is located on a rocky peninsula on the rugged east coast of New Zealand's south island, it stands out from lush farmland between the mountains. This small village became a popular tourist destination, mainly because of the possibility to observe wild animals. Few places can show such a variety of wildlife to be observed in the wild. Whales, dolphins, seals, penguins and albatrosses are regularly sighted in this area, making it a true nature lover dream. Visitors can either take part in the Art Trail and watch the craftsmen at work in their workshops, or visit the city’s first house, which was built in 1842, is very well preserved and its foundation is surprisingly built from whale bones. So if you are looking for history, craft culture and lots of outdoor activities, Kaikoura will certainly not disappoint you. Overnight in Kaikoura.

Day 12 Picton at Marlborough Sound

The port city of Picton, originally Waitohi, with almost 3,000 inhabitants, is located at the end of one of the fjord-like coves of Marlborough Sound, Queen Charlotte Sound in the region of Marlborough in the north of the south island of New Zealand. Picton was named after Thomas Picton, a military ally of Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington, who fell in 1815 at the Battle of Waterloo. The city serves as a ferry port for the connecting route between the North and South Islands of New Zealand, which extends from Wellington via the Cook Strait. In 2000, the port of Picton was extended by the Waimahara Wharf in the immediately adjacent Shakespeare Bay. Apart from that, Picton also has a great importance for New Zealand tourism as a starting point for many trips in Marlborough Sound. Picton attractions include the three-master Edwin Fox, located in the port at the Edwin Fox Maritime Museum, as well as a small museum dedicated to whales (with information about whaling) overnight in Picton.

Day 13 The Kapiti Coast on the North Island

The ride on the Interislander ferry is very impressive and offers many beautiful photo motifs. First, glide through the Marlborough Sounds, an area of “drunken” valleys created by the geological subsidence of a central mountain range. The original mountain range is still evidenced by steep mountain ridges, which today spring out as narrow peninsulas between deep sea arms. The steep banks have a fjord-like character in places. Salmon and mussels are bred in the sunden - in addition to wine production, the most important industry in the region. You will then pass through the Cook Waterway, which separates the North Island from the South Island, and arrive at Wellington Harbour. Here in Wellington you have the chance to explore a Sanctuary in the middle of the city with a guide, as well as the city itself. In the late afternoon you will drive along the Kapiti coast and stay overnight on the Kapiti Coast. Overnight in Waikanae.

14th day Tongariro National Park

Tongariro National Park is the oldest national park in New Zealand and of volcanic origin.The wise foresight of Maori chief Te Heuheu Tukino laid the foundation for the establishment of the fourth-oldest national park in the world. Tongariro National Park is defined by three sister volcanoes, of which the Mt Ruapehu (2796 m) is the highest and most active. The park, which extends into the center of the North Island, is one of only 23 sites on both UNESCO’s World Heritage and Cultural Heritage list. On the territory of the national park are several places of worship of the Maori, who venerate the volcanoes as sacred. Overnight in Ohakune.

Day 15 Enjoy Tongariro National Park

Take a look around the national park in peace and enjoy the unique nature. Overnight in Ohakune.

Day 16 - From Tongariro to Rotorua's Geysers

Today you have another opportunity for a natural history hike and circle the beautiful lake Rotopounamu at the foot of the Tongariro volcano. Then you pass the Taupo Lake and drive to Rotorua. Rotorua is known for the many hot springs, geysers and mud volcanoes that occur there. Rotorua’s history began many years ago, when the Maori settled in this area favored by nature. Here they found a large lake, besides hot springs and geysers. The white settlers also appreciated Rotorua’s thermal water with its rheumatic healing effect. Rotorua was declared a spa town in 1901, and since then the city has been a draw for visitors from all over the world. Everywhere, craters seem to growl, mud ponds blubber, and every now and then a huge water fountain (geyser) shoots up a few meters - a rare, fascinating natural spectacle! Here you can feel the "pulse of the earth" up close and the sulfur smell over Rotorua proves that the earth's surface here is only very thin. Overnight in Rotorua.

18.-19. Tag Coromandel Peninsula

Along the Bay of Plenty, past the Tauranga harbor city with the distinctive volcanic cone of Mount Maunganui and the gold mining town of Waihi, you can reach Tairua on the Coromandel Peninsula. The Coromandel Peninsula (Maori: Te Tara-o-te-ika-o-Maui) is located on the North Island of New Zealand. It is 85 kilometers long and 40 km wide. Located west of the Bay of Plenty, it partially delimits the western Hauraki Gulf with the Firth of Thames from the Pacific Ocean. New Zealand's largest city, Auckland, lies on the opposite side of the Hauraki Gulf, 55 kilometers to the west. In good weather, especially in winter, the peninsula is easily recognizable from the city. The name comes from the merchant ship HMS Coromandel (1820), which in turn was named after the Indian Coromandel Coast. Overnight in Whitianga.

Day 20 Coromandel Peninsula - Auckland

You will leave the Coromandel Peninsula and reach Auckland. During the tour to your hotel you will get acquainted with the "city of sails", as Auckland is also called. You can ride on the extinct volcanic cone of Mount Eden, from where you have a beautiful view over the city. Or to the elegant suburb of "Parnell" and on to the port of Auckland. Auckland with its suburbs has about 1 million. Residents. It is the largest city in the country and spreads generously over a narrow landing strait between the Pacific Ocean and Tasman Sea. Over 60 extinct volcanic cones and craters characterize the landscape, while an endless procession of sails in the harbor is typical for the relaxed way of life here. Auckland was the state capital until 1865 and is now the economic center of New Zealand. Overnight in Auckland.

21st day Delivery of the rental car and return or onward flight

Today your New Zealand trip ends. You drive to the airport and drop off the rental car at the car rental company.
